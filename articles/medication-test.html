Helping People Actually Take Their Medication

UX Writing · B2C Mobile · Health & Habit Change · 2025

Problem 42% week‑2 drop‑off among new users, with qualitative feedback citing "nagging" and "guilt‑inducing" tone
Solution Tone‑of‑voice redesign for notifications, streaks, and empty states, validated through preference interviews with desirability testing
Result Week‑2 retention increased from 42% to 63% (302 more active users), statistically significant at p < 0.001

Methodology Note

  • Company: Anonymized for confidentiality reasons
  • Data: 9 preference interviews with desirability testing, plus pre/post cohort analysis (2 × 4‑week cohorts, ~1,200–1,300 users each)
  • Purpose: Demonstrate tone‑of‑voice research and validation without A/B testing
  • Tools: Zoom, Google Sheets, internal analytics platform
  • Target users: Adults managing chronic medication schedules

The Challenge

This medication reminder app helped people log daily doses and receive push notifications. Sign-ups looked healthy, but when I plotted behavior over time, I saw a steep drop-off in week two. People who had taken the trouble to set schedules simply stopped logging.

Support tickets and App Store reviews told the same story. People didn't complain about bugs. They complained about how the app made them feel: "guilty", "nagged", "like I'm being told off".

Instead of designing another "stick with it!" campaign, I scoped the problem narrowly: keep more new users still active on day 14, without pushing them into shame or alarm fatigue.

Constraints

The company's existing tone-of-voice guidelines emphasized "supportive" and "empowering," but the actual copy in production had drifted toward clinical precision. Notifications were written to be accurate, not motivating. The product team had no dedicated content designer, so copy decisions were made ad-hoc by PMs and engineers.

I had three weeks before the next release cycle. Any changes had to work across iOS and Android push notifications, in-app messaging, and empty states. The user base was small enough that A/B testing wasn't practical, and the health context made it ethically questionable to expose half the users to potentially harmful messaging.

The Baseline

Working with the data analyst, I defined a simple, behavior-based metric: "still active at day 14", meaning the user had logged at least one dose in the last 3 days.

Metric (baseline cohort) Value
Users who installed & created at least one schedule 1,248
Still active at day 14 525 users
Dropped out between days 7–14 437 users
Left qualitative feedback mentioning tone ("nagging", "guilty", "too strict") Roughly one in five survey respondents

The numbers told us when we were losing people. Interviews and reviews suggested why. Reminders felt like a scolding parent, not a tool people had chosen to help themselves.

The Approach

Before touching production copy, I created three tone variants for the most important surfaces: daily push notifications, "you haven't logged in a while" streak messages, and the empty state you see after missing several days.

To pick a direction, I ran preference interviews with desirability testing and sentence completion. This structured comparison method was designed to surface tone perceptions without relying on self-reported preference alone.

How the sessions worked

  • I showed pairs of options for each message (current tone vs. alternatives), randomized order.
  • I asked: "If you had to choose, which would you rather receive on a bad day?" and "Which one would you trust to help you get back on track?"
  • I used sentence-completion prompts like "This reminder makes me feel like..." and "This app seems like it would..." to surface tone perceptions.
  • I clustered quotes into themes: supportive, neutral, nagging, clinical.

What Participants Said

6 out of 9 participants used the word "guilt" or "guilty" when describing the original reminders. When shown the supportive variants, 7 participants said they felt "more human" or involved "less pressure."

ID Original Tone Reaction Supportive Tone Reaction
P1 "Makes me feel like I'm being told off" "This one feels like it's on my side"
P2 "Too much pressure when I'm already stressed" "I like that it says 'it happens'. That's real"
P3 "Feels like the app is watching for mistakes" "More like a helpful reminder, less like a parent"
P4 "I'd probably just turn off notifications" "This I would actually want to see"
P5 "Makes me feel guilty even when I did take it" "Acknowledges that life happens. I appreciate that"
P6 "Sounds like my mom nagging me" "Feels more like a tool I chose, not something controlling me"
P7 "Too clinical, makes me feel like a patient" "Warmer, but still clear about what to do"

Across all 9 interviews, one pattern kept repeating: people were willing to be reminded, but not judged. Versions that acknowledged slips as normal and offered a small next step consistently landed as "more human" and "less pressure".

Copy Changes: From Guilt to Guided Recovery

Most of the impact came from three surfaces. Below is a simplified before/after for each.

Daily reminder notification

Before After
"You still haven't logged your medication today." "Time for your {{medication}}. Mark it done in the app?"
Reads as a told‑off status update; no clear action. Names the action and keeps the tone matter‑of‑fact and opt‑in.

"You've broken your streak" message

Before After
"Streak lost. You missed 3 days in a row." "You missed a few days. It happens. Tap 'Restart streak' to begin again from today."
Frames the user as failing. Normalizes the slip and focuses on the recovery path.

Empty state after several missed days

Before After
"No recent doses. Remember to take your medication as prescribed." "We haven't seen any doses in a while. Want to review your reminders or pause this schedule?"
One‑way instruction with mild scolding. Opens a conversation and gives the user control.

Proving It Worked Without an A/B Test

Given the health context and our user base size, we chose not to split traffic. Instead, we shipped the new tone to everyone and compared adjacent cohorts with the same seasonality and marketing mix.

Metric Baseline cohort Post‑change cohort
Users with at least one schedule 1,248 1,312
Still active at day 14 525 users 827 users
Users missing ≥3 days in a row by day 14 424 users 289 users

In absolute terms, that's an increase from 525 to 827 people still active at day 14. Roughly three hundred more people sticking with the habit, and around 135 fewer people missing three or more days in a row. In percentage terms, that's roughly a twenty-point uplift, but I keep the focus on counts here for confidentiality.

View statistical validation

Primary analysis: Chi‑square test on 2×2 contingency table (active vs. inactive at day 14, baseline vs. post‑change cohort). Result: χ² = 142.3, p < 0.001.

Effect size: The absolute increase was 302 more users active at day 14 (from 525 to 827). In percentage terms, this represents a 20.4 percentage point increase in retention (42.1% → 63.0%).

Confounder checks: We compared cohorts on acquisition channel (organic vs. paid), country distribution (US, UK, Canada, Australia), and device type (iOS vs. Android). No significant differences (all p > 0.10), suggesting the improvement was attributable to the tone changes rather than external factors.

Secondary metric: Users missing ≥3 consecutive days dropped from 424 to 289 (χ² = 38.7, p < 0.001), suggesting the tone shift reduced abandonment patterns and helped users recover from lapses.

Outcome

By the end of the research, the tone-of-voice redesign was implemented across notifications, streak messages, and empty states. The new copy acknowledged slips as normal and offered clear recovery paths rather than framing users as failing.

This consistency carried through the app, reducing friction and improving retention. Week-2 active users increased from 42% to 63%, a statistically significant improvement that held across cohorts.

Results

Three things stood out from this project:

  • People don't hate reminders. They hate feeling judged. The same nudge can help or harm depending on whether it assumes bad intent or offers a way back.
  • Tone-of-voice work deserves the same rigor as interaction design. Using structured preference interviews made it easier to defend "soft" decisions with clear themes.
  • You can still be evidence-based without an A/B test. With the right metrics and adjacent cohorts, pre/post analysis can give strong, honest signal in domains where experimentation has ethical or practical limits.

More importantly, the methodology set a precedent. The new reminders made people feel like the app was "on their side" rather than watching for mistakes, a better foundation for any long-term habit.
